# What AI Is Straining or Breaking in InnerSource: A Synthesis

Ask twenty practitioners across banking, telecom, automotive, industrial manufacturing, and tech what AI is doing to their InnerSource practice, and one answer comes back almost everywhere, unprompted: **review capacity has become the bottleneck.** An engineer at a UK-based bank's Open Source Program Office laid it out most cleanly: AI can now increase contribution volume faster than any organization increases its contribution quality controls. That single sentence is close to the whole survey in miniature.

The consensus isn't just "we get more pull requests." It's a specific, mechanical failure mode: **the cost of producing a contribution has dropped to near zero, but the cost of reviewing one hasn't moved.** A contributor at a large industrial engineering firm put it bluntly: AI-generated code is often harder to read than human-written code, more verbose than it needs to be, and it keeps arriving faster than a normal reviewer has time to absorb. A telecom engineer went further, arguing that in a world where AI can regenerate anything on demand, reuse itself starts to feel unnecessary, "trivial." That's not a fringe view. It shows up, in softer language, in reply after reply.

Underneath the volume problem sits a second, harder one: **trust has lost its old anchor.** InnerSource, like open source before it, was built on a specific kind of trust: you trusted a contribution because you trusted the person who stood behind it. A financial-services architect named this precisely: a contribution can look polished, compile cleanly, and pass every test, and still reflect weak engineering judgment. What a reviewer needs now isn't confidence in the diff. It's confidence that whoever is accountable for it actually understands it. That's a much harder thing to verify at scale, and almost nobody in the survey claims to have solved it. The clearest attempt to solve it: that same respondent's own five-part breakdown of the strain, splitting it cleanly into review capacity, trust, ownership, governance, and consistency, and treating each as a distinct discipline rather than one blob called "governance."

Two responses sharpen this into something genuinely uncomfortable, worth building the talk's middle around. First: a large enterprise software vendor reported a case where a high-profile internal project **closed its source entirely** because it lacked the capacity or maturity to handle the inflow of AI-generated pull requests. Not degraded. Not slowed down. Closed. That is the review-capacity strain taken to its logical, worst-case conclusion, and it happened, not hypothetically.

Second, and more counterintuitive: over-governing can produce the *same* failure as under-governing. A Brazilian e-commerce company, speaking anonymously, described exactly this trap: their review and governance processes, tightened in direct response to AI-generated contributions, became so bureaucratic that teams facing that friction chose to duplicate a solution from scratch rather than navigate contributing it back. The instinct to protect InnerSource by adding more governance around AI contributions can, if miscalibrated, produce the very sprawl it was meant to prevent. Governance isn't a dial you can just turn up.

That tension is where a bank technologist takes the argument somewhere almost nobody else does. Trust, in their framing, was never really about verifying artifacts. It was about human judgment, exercised collectively, about what a project should be and where it should go. They reach for an unlikely comparison: watching a live performance where music, visual art, and politics fused into something that was, in total, more than any single element, a distinctly human experience no machine could produce alone. Their conclusion: when agents write and review the code itself, governance becomes the place where human judgment still has to live. Not a checklist. Not a policy document. The actual site where humans decide who to trust, what "good" means, and who is accountable when something breaks. **Governance as art. Art as governance,** in their words.

Put together, the pattern is this: AI hasn't broken InnerSource's principles, but it has broken the assumption that human review capacity would scale roughly with contribution volume, an assumption InnerSource quietly depended on for twenty years. Every organization here is now improvising a fix, whether that's arming reviewers with AI of their own, redefining what "ownership" means for an AI-assisted change, or, in the worst case, closing the door rather than solve the problem. The one thing nobody in this data set believes: that AI removes the need for InnerSource discipline. Nearly every respondent says, in their own words, the opposite. It raises the price of getting governance right.
